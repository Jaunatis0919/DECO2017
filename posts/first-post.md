Blog 1: Deconstructing the Brief and Navigating the "Grief-Tech" Landscape

1. Interpreting the Brief: Core Functional Requirements
The initial phase of our Full-Stack project begins with a fundamental question: What constitutes a community platform? Based on my interpretation of the project brief, a functional web application prototype must address three hierarchical layers:

Content (The Core): A mechanism for users to 'post' and 'consume' information. This is the non-negotiable foundation of any digital exchange.

Interaction (The Experience): Moving beyond passive reading to active engagement—features like commenting, liking, or bookmarking. While not all are required, at least one is essential to bridge the gap between a static site and a social entity.

Community Logic (The Structure): A system that organizes content through thematic categorization or user-relationship dynamics (e.g., feeds or tags).

2. Conceptualization: Why "Loss" and "Grief"?
Personal experiences with the "finality of loss"—whether through the passing of loved ones or the drifting apart of significant relationships—have deeply shaped my understanding of social support. I have found that while loss is a solitary pain, the path to resilience is often paved by the "Intervention of the Other’s Perspective."

Secondary research into Ambiguous Loss and Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) suggests that the goal of grieving is not "closure," but "meaning-making." Current mainstream social media platforms, however, suffer from a "positivity bias." The pressure to maintain a curated, successful digital persona creates a gap where intense, negative emotions are suppressed. We see a clear opportunity for a "Safe Space" that facilitates Digital Mourning—a space where rituals (like the digital equivalent of lighting a candle) follow the structuralist logic of symbolic exchange.

3. Technical Feasibility and Identified Risks
From a Full-Stack perspective, building a platform for such sensitive content involves several technical and ethical considerations:

Safety & Privacy: How do we ensure a "safe" environment? We need to prioritize robust data handling and perhaps "lightweight" user relationships to prevent the performative toxicity found on other platforms.

Feasibility: Using our current understanding of the MERN stack (or similar), implementing a basic CRUD (Create, Read, Update, Delete) system for "tributes" or "reflections" is achievable. The challenge lies in the ERD (Entity Relationship Diagram) design—mapping how a user interacts with a "memorial" or "shared grief thread" without overcomplicating the database.

4. Shared Vision and Strategic Timeline
The most rewarding part of this early stage was establishing a common vision within my group. We agreed that this project should transcend a mere academic exercise and become a meaningful tool. This alignment has built immediate trust, allowing us to set a rigorous timeline:

Week 6: Deep dive into grief psychology and competitive analysis of "Grief-Tech."

Week 7: Requirements finalization, sketching, and defining User Flows.

Week 8-9: Wireframe iterations and defining data requirements via ERDs and DDDs.

Week 10: Final architectural refinement focused on conceptual integrity.

By grounding our technical decisions in human-centric needs, we aim to transform the "functional requirements" of a blog assignment into a prototype for digital healing.
